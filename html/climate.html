Yosemite National Park

Yosemite National Park is being greatly damaged by Climate Change. Yosemite's largest ice fields, the Lyell and Maclure glaciers, have already lost roughly 90% of their ice volume over the last century due to rising temperatures. Prolonged, severe droughts combined with massive bark beetle infestations have killed millions of trees, fundamentally altering the park's dense conifer forests. Additionally, hotter and drier conditions have fueled increasingly intense wildfires that have repeatedly threatened Yosemite's ancient giant sequoia groves.

Scientists project that the park's remaining glaciers will disappear entirely within the next few decades, permanently altering the mountain hydrology. As the Sierra Nevada snowpack melts earlier each spring, Yosemite will experience intensely dry, water-scarce summers that threaten the survival of its river ecosystems and famous waterfalls. Furthermore, rising temperatures will force high-altitude species, like the American pika, further up the mountainsides until they run out of elevation and face local extinction.

Actions to Minimize Climate Impact

  • Accelerate prescribed fires and strategic forest thinning to mimic natural fire cycles and reduce catastrophic wildfire fuel.
  • Restore subalpine meadows and wetland corridors to retain seasonal snowmelt, recharge groundwater, and build drought resilience.
  • Transition park operations and gateway communities entirely to renewable energy, microgrids, and zero-emission shuttle fleets.
  • Protect critical ecological corridors to give climate-vulnerable wildlife room to shift to higher, cooler elevations.

Local Efforts and Limitations

  • Park managers and local tribes are actively applying cultural and prescribed burns around giant sequoia groves to clear dense fuels and protect ancient trees.
  • Yosemite and surrounding gateway towns are expanding solar installations, upgrading energy efficiency, and running electric shuttles to curb local emissions.
  • Complete mitigation remains difficult because local actions cannot halt global warming, strict regulatory windows limit safe burn days, and chronic underfunding restricts large-scale restoration projects.
